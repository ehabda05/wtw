# Problem evidence: verified statistics for the pitch

HackHarvard 2026. Researched and accessed 2026-10-16.

**Standard used:** a number is VERIFIED only if we read it in the original report, dataset, or peer-reviewed paper, not in a news article about it. Numbers marked *(derived)* are our own arithmetic from verified source values. Say "derived" in speaker notes, and put the raw source values on the slide.

**Pitch beats these support:**
1. Friends drift apart (time with friends is collapsing).
2. Plans die in the group chat (nobody feels responsible).
3. The organizer burns out (initiative is lopsided).
4. Unanswered messages feel like rejection.

## Recommended top 3 for the deck

| # | Where | Slide line (our words) | Footer citation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Slide 2 (problem)** | Americans aged 15-24 spent **153 min/day** with friends in person in 2003, **67** in 2019 and **43** in 2020. | Kannan & Veazie, *SSM-Population Health* 2023 (ATUS 2003-2020), suppl. table for Fig. 4 |
| 2 | Group-chat beat | Ask one person and **36%** reply. Ask the same thing with 14 or 49 others copied and **14%** reply. | Blair, Thompson & Wuensch, *Basic & Applied Soc. Psych.* 27(2), 2005, p. 175 |
| 3 | Organizer beat | In the average relationship, one person is **almost twice as likely** to start contact. Long one-sided streaks make the relationship more likely to end. | Mollgaard & Mathiesen, *PLoS ONE* 11(4) e0154442, 2016, Fig. 2-3 |

**Alternate for #3 (the "feels like rejection" beat):** when a message was read but got no reply, **42.8%** of senders described feeling upset or angry, and **26.9%** felt slighted or ignored. Source: Hoyle et al., CHI 2017, Table 3, N=402.

**Slide-2 variant for an all-ages audience:** "Americans' in-person time with friends fell from 60 to 34 min/day (2003 to 2019), then to 20 in 2020." Same source, Sec. 3.2 / Fig. 1.

## Bench: verified but not ranked

- **BLS ATUS 2025 (newest official data).** In 2025, socializing and communicating averaged **35 min/day**, down from **41 min in 2015**. The share doing any on an average day fell from **38% to 30%**.
  - Source: BLS release USDL-26-1022, 25 Jun 2026, Leisure section, tables 1 and 11A. Covers ages 15+; about 6,100 people interviewed in 2025.
  - Status: VERIFIED.
  - Caution: this counts socializing as a primary activity with anyone, not time with friends. BLS publishes no official friend-time series. Use it only if someone asks for the newest year.
- **Smith & Williams (2004), "R U There? Ostracism by Cell Phone Text Messages,"** *Group Dynamics* 8(4):291-301.
  - Finding: in a 3-person text chat, participants got no replies for **8 minutes**. They then reported lower belonging (d=1.61), control (d=1.28), self-esteem (d=0.96) and meaningful existence, compared with included participants. Results on p. 297.
  - Sample: N=40 Australian undergraduates.
  - Status: VERIFIED.
  - Caution: small sample, 2004, lab setting with confederates. It is causal evidence that being ignored in a group text hurts, so it is good for speaker notes.
- **Cigna Group, "Loneliness in America 2025".** **67%** of Gen Z (born 1997-2006) classify as lonely, versus 65% of millennials, 60% of Gen X and 44% of boomers. Report p. 5.
  - Data: 2024 Vitality Index, fielded 29 May-14 Jun 2024 with 5,000+ US adults by the Evernorth Research Institute with Morning Consult.
  - Status: VERIFIED.
  - Caution: "Gen Z" here means ages about 18-27, not 18-24, and the report does not name the loneliness scale. The "57%" and "52%" all-adult figures in news coverage are not in this report.
- **Liu, Rim, Min & Min (2023), "The surprise of reaching out: Appreciated more than we think,"** *JPSP* 124(4):754-771, doi 10.1037/pspi0000402.
  - Finding (from the abstract): across preregistered experiments, people underestimate how much others appreciate being reached out to. The gap is larger when the reach-out is more surprising or the people are more distant.
  - Status: VERIFIED qualitatively. No number verified.
  - Use: supports the agent nudging quiet members ("they'll appreciate it more than you think").
- **Rosenthal (1985), "Kinkeeping in the Familial Division of Labor,"** *J. Marriage and the Family* 47(4):965-974.
  - Finding (from the abstract): more than half of respondents said someone in their extended family works at keeping members in touch. Kinkeeping is mainly done by women and is often passed from mother to daughter.
  - Sample: stratified random sample of adults 40+ in Hamilton, Ontario.
  - Status: VERIFIED, abstract level.
  - Caution: **family only**. We found no peer-reviewed study that measures the "friend-group organizer" burden directly. Use it as an analogy: "families have kinkeepers; friend groups have that one organizer".
- **Rose (1984), "How Friendships End: Patterns among Young Adults,"** *JSPR* 1(3):267-277.
  - Finding (from the abstract): physical separation is one of four ways friendships end. The others are new friends, growing dislike, and dating or marriage.
  - Status: VERIFIED qualitatively. It gives no share, so do not claim "most friendships end from distance".
